What is OrthoGather

OrthoGather is a local Flask web app that combines OrthoFinder (orthology inference) with GOATOOLS (Gene Ontology enrichment). It lets you compare protein sets across many species (UpSet plots, shared/unique orthogroups, exports) and then ask functional questions over the same dataset: what biological processes are enriched in this subset?

Everything runs on your machine; no data leaves your laptop. The only outgoing traffic is downloading reference proteomes (UniProt) and GO annotation files (GOA), and the GO basic ontology.

Quick start: your first analysis

From the home page you have three entry points (in the same order as the cards on the landing page):

New Analysis

Start from scratch. Search UniProt's proteome catalogue, tick the species you want, and OrthoGather downloads the FASTAs and runs OrthoFinder locally.

Preselected Dataset

Try a curated example with 47 bacterial species. Best to learn the interface without waiting for OrthoFinder to finish.

External Upload

Already have orthogroups? Drop an OrthoFinder ZIP, or an OrthoXML file from OrthoDB / OMA / eggNOG / Ensembl Compara, and skip straight to the analysis screens.

Heads up: running OrthoFinder on more than ~10 species can take tens of minutes. Use the History page to re-open completed runs without recomputing.

External Upload: ZIP or OrthoXML

The External Upload entry point accepts two formats:

  • OrthoFinder ZIP: the output folder of an OrthoFinder run, zipped (must contain Orthogroups.tsv and Orthogroups.GeneCount.tsv at the top level).
  • OrthoXML (.xml or .orthoxml): the Quest for Orthologs (QfO) standard for representing orthology relationships. Exported by OrthoDB, OMA, eggNOG, Ensembl Compara, PANTHER, InParanoid and OrthoFinder itself.

With OrthoXML support you can plug in precomputed orthogroups from any of these databases without rerunning OrthoFinder locally. Useful when:

  • you have an OrthoDB .orthoxml covering hundreds of species and just want to do UpSet plots / GO enrichment on a subset;
  • you want to compare two orthology methods (e.g. OrthoFinder vs OMA) on the same species set — upload each one separately;
  • you trust an external curation pipeline more than what OrthoFinder would produce on your laptop.

How it works under the hood: OrthoGather parses the XML, flattens any nested <paralogGroup> blocks (every gene inside an <orthologGroup> ends up in the same orthogroup row), and writes the same Orthogroups.tsv / Orthogroups.GeneCount.tsv files that OrthoFinder produces. Everything downstream (UpSet plots, GO enrichment, history, compare, citations) works identically.

Protein Analysis page

After a successful run you land on Protein Analysis. The page is organised top-to-bottom as a series of figures with an action bar above each.

Figures 1 & 2: orthogroup landscape

  • Figure 1: distribution of orthogroup sizes (number of proteins per orthogroup).
  • Figure 2: orthogroup sharing across species (how many orthogroups are present in 1, 2, 3, … species).

Throughout OrthoGather we count proteins, not genes. OrthoFinder operates on protein FASTA sequences, and an orthogroup is a set of proteins inferred to descend from a common ancestral sequence. The two terms are sometimes used interchangeably in the GO literature, but in this app "size of an orthogroup" always means "number of proteins it contains".

Both are interactive Plotly charts. Hover for exact counts; use each figure's Image menu (PNG at 2× scale, or SVG vector) to export, or the Data menu to grab the underlying table as Excel/CSV/TSV/JSON.

Species picker & action bar

Below Figure 2, the Compare specific species card holds a chip-based species picker. Narrow the list with the Filter species… box, then click a chip to include a species in the comparison; click again to remove it. A counter shows how many are selected, and Clear resets the selection. These chips are the same ones that drive Figures 3, 4, 5 and 6.

Once at least two species are selected, click Generate UpSet figures to render Figures 3 and 4. There is no separate "save" or "share" button: each figure instead carries its own Data menu (Excel, CSV, TSV, JSON), an Image menu (PNG at 2× scale, or SVG vector) and a full-screen button.

Figures 3 and 4: UpSet plots

UpSet plots show set intersections without the visual chaos of large Venn diagrams. Each row at the bottom-left is a species; the matrix in the middle shows which combination ("which species share this set?"); the bar at the top is the count. OrthoGather draws two complementary views:

  • Figure 3 — UpSet of shared orthogroups: each bar counts the orthogroups in that species combination.
  • Figure 4 — UpSet of shared proteins: the same intersections, but weighted by total proteins rather than orthogroup count.

Hover a bar to see the exact count, click to drill into that intersection. Use each figure's Image menu (PNG/SVG) to download the figure, or the Data menu to grab the underlying table as Excel/CSV/TSV/JSON.

Figures 5 and 6: UniProt filter

Paste UniProt accession IDs in the filter box and Figures 5 / 6 show only orthogroups that contain at least one of your IDs. Useful when you have a hit list and want to know which species share each hit's orthogroup.

Gene Ontology page

The Gene Ontology page (top-right tab in Protein Analysis, or the Run GO enrichment button at the bottom of the page) walks you through five steps. Each step unlocks when the previous one succeeds.

1. Species match

OrthoGather looks up every species in your OrthoFinder dataset against the UniProt proteome catalogue, so it knows which GOA file to download.

Common abbreviations like E. coli, S. cerevisiae, D. melanogaster, C. elegans are expanded automatically. If a row shows No match, use the Fix field. You can paste either an NCBI Taxon ID (e.g. 83333) or the full species name (e.g. Escherichia coli K-12), and OrthoGather will retry the match.

What the two columns mean

The table has two independent checks per row. Read them as a pair:

  • Matched entry in catalogue (green tick with the proteome label): a UniProt proteome exists for this species. The match is good.
  • GOA file (green tick): the matched proteome also has a GO annotation file published by EBI/UniProt-GOA. This is what OrthoGather will download for enrichment.

A species can be matched but still have no GOA file (green name on the left, red on the right). This happens when UniProt knows the proteome exists but UniProt-GOA hasn't curated GO annotations for it yet, which is common for non-model organisms (environmental Burkholderia, exotic Mycobacterium, clinical isolates, etc.). It is a data-source limitation, not a bug in OrthoGather. Those species will still appear in the orthogroup tables but contribute no GO annotations during enrichment.

2. Download GOA

Clicking Download GOA files pulls the matched annotation files from EBI and builds a summary listing every orthogroup, its members, and their annotation status. Export it from the Download GOA tables control as Excel (Gene_Ontology_Analysis.xlsx), CSV or TSV — the on-disk record of which proteins carry GO terms.

Once the download finishes, an Annotation overview panel appears: a quick sanity check on how well-annotated your dataset is, with a distribution chart of the share of proteins per orthogroup that carry GO terms. Use it to judge whether an enrichment will have enough signal before you run it.

GOA, GAF, UniProt: who provides what?

  • UniProt publishes the protein sequences (the FASTAs that OrthoFinder consumes) and curates protein metadata.
  • GAF (Gene Association Format) is a tab-separated file format defined by the GO Consortium. It has 17 columns: protein ID, GO term, evidence code, namespace, etc.
  • GOA (Gene Ontology Annotation) is the dataset published by the EBI UniProt-GOA project, a collaboration between EBI/UniProt and the GO Consortium. The files are distributed in GAF format, with extension .goa or .gaf. OrthoGather downloads them from ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/GO/goa/proteomes/.

In short: GAF is the format, GOA is the dataset, and both come from the EBI/UniProt-GOA project.

3. Foreground

The foreground is your hit list: the proteins you want to enrich. Paste UniProt IDs (one per line or space-separated; FASTA headers are accepted too).

Tick Expand to full orthogroups to broaden the test: any orthogroup that contains one of your hits contributes all its members to the foreground. This is the heart of orthogroup-based propagation. You're asking "what functions are enriched across these protein families?" rather than "what functions are enriched in this exact set of human proteins?".

4. Background

The background is the universe against which enrichment is tested. Three common choices:

  • "Use all GOA files": the simplest default, every protein in every species' GOA file. Good when your foreground could have come from anywhere.
  • Custom IDs without orthogroup expansion: paste a restricted list (e.g. only kinases). Use when your experiment was technically restricted.
  • Custom IDs with orthogroup expansion: same idea, but propagated across orthogroups. Symmetrical to the foreground.

5. Analysis settings & run

The Analysis settings card exposes three stringency choices. Defaults are sensible for most users; adjust knowingly.

Evidence quality

  • All: every annotation, including IEA (Inferred from Electronic Annotation). Most permissive. The vast majority of annotations in any GOA file are IEA. They propagate by sequence similarity from a small number of curated annotations.
  • Curated: keeps human-curated entries plus IBA (Inferred from Biological aspect of Ancestor, i.e. PAINT-curated phylogenetic propagation done by GO Consortium biocurators). A defensible middle ground that includes high-quality computational inference.
  • Experimental only: strictest. Just experimental codes (EXP, IDA, IPI, IMP, IGI, IEP) and high-throughput experimental codes (HEP, HDA, HMP, HGI). Expect fewer hits but very high confidence.

When to use which? If you study a single well-annotated model organism (human, mouse, yeast), Experimental only often gives a cleaner story. If you study a non-model species, Curated lets you leverage PAINT-derived inferences without drowning in IEA noise.

Orthogroups

  • All orthogroups: every OG that contains at least one foreground hit is propagated. Maximises recall.
  • Single-copy only: restrict to OGs with exactly one protein per species (the safest 1:1:1 orthology relationships). More conservative but immune to paralog confusion.

Counting

  • Per protein (default): every protein in the propagated foreground counts as one observation. Compatible with most published enrichment workflows.
  • Per orthogroup: collapse each OG to a single observation (one anchor protein per OG). Mitigates pseudoreplication: an OG with 10 species members would otherwise contribute 10 near-identical observations.

Reading the output

After clicking Run GO enrichment the page reveals four sections, top to bottom: a coverage banner, the Plotly bar chart, the re-run bar, and the interactive table.

Enriched vs depleted: the test is two-sided, so a term can be enriched (▲ over-represented in your foreground) or depleted (▼ purified, under-represented). The chart bars show magnitude only, so depleted terms are drawn in a distinct warm colour and flagged in the Direction column of the table — a tall bar is never silently read as "enriched". The results table is headed "Enriched & depleted terms" for this reason.

Coverage banner

The first thing you see is a four-column banner that summarises how usable your foreground is:

  • You pasted: the raw count of UniProt IDs you submitted.
  • After orthogroup expansion (only shown when you ticked "Expand to full orthogroups"): the propagated foreground size, i.e. how many proteins the test actually runs on.
  • With GO annotations: the subset that has at least one GO term in the background, plus the percentage. Below 30% triggers a warning note; above 60% is "good coverage".
  • Background universe: the total size of the universe the hypergeometric test runs against.

Bar chart

  • Namespace tabs (All / BP / CC / MF) at the top-left. "All" overlays the three namespaces; the individual tabs zoom into one.
  • "Top N" selector at the top-right (default 20 per namespace). The chart caps at the top-N most significant terms so the figure stays readable; raise the selector to 50 / 100 / All, or use the full table below. When truncated, a note below the chart tells you "Showing top 60 of 696 terms".
  • Hover any bar for the full term name, GO ID, depth, study / population counts, and exact p-value.
  • PNG and SVG download buttons on the right. The PNG is scaled 2× for crisp output; the SVG is publication-ready vector graphics.

Re-run bar

Sits directly under the chart so you can tighten the analysis without scrolling. Tweak Min depth, P-value (FDR), or Max terms per NS and click Re-run with new thresholds. Each input has a ? popover explaining when to use it.

Interactive table

  • Column tooltips: every header has a "(?)" hint. Hover to see what the column means (e.g. "study_count / study_n: how many of your foreground proteins are annotated with this term, out of how many were tested").
  • Sort: click any column header to sort ascending / descending.
  • Direction column: each row is tagged ▲ enriched or ▼ depleted, matching the warm-coloured bars in the chart above.
  • Filter: search box (free text on GO ID + name), namespace chips (All / BP / CC / MF), FDR and Depth numeric inputs all update the table live.
  • "Show" selector: 10 / 20 / 50 / 100 / All rows on screen. Default 20 so the page doesn't dump 700 rows by default. A counter below the toolbar shows "Showing 20 of 696 terms" so you know what you're looking at.
  • Evidence chips, colour-coded by GO Consortium tier: green = experimental (EXP, IDA, IPI, IMP, IGI, IEP and high-throughput variants), blue = curated (TAS, NAS, IC, IBA / PAINT), and gray = computational (IEA, ISS and other sequence-similarity codes). Hover any chip for the code name and count.
  • Export: CSV / TSV / JSON of the currently filtered rows (i.e. only what the table is showing), or the full Excel of every significant term from the GOATOOLS run.

History & reproducibility

Every completed analysis is recorded in History. From there you can:

  • Reproduce: re-open the run with its original species, OrthoFinder parameters and selection state.
  • Cite: export a BibTeX or RIS entry with the exact OrthoGather version and date.
  • Compare: load two runs side-by-side to see differences in orthogroup composition, foreground hits, etc.
  • Rename / delete: keep your history tidy.

Frequently asked questions

Why didn't E. coli match in the species table?

OrthoGather automatically expands common Latin abbreviations (E. coli → Escherichia coli, S. cerevisiae → Saccharomyces cerevisiae, etc.). If a row still shows "No match", the species name might be ambiguous or unusual. Type either the NCBI Taxon ID (e.g. 83333) or the full Latin name in the Fix field and click "Fix".

What is orthogroup-based propagation?

When you tick "Expand to full orthogroups", OrthoGather pulls in every other member of each foreground orthogroup (across all species in your analysis) and unions their GO annotations. This is how a protein in a poorly annotated species can "inherit" GO terms from its well-annotated orthologs.

Note: this is propagation by union, not by majority vote. If 9 out of 10 orthologs say "DNA repair" and 1 says "kinase activity", the OG gets both annotations. If you want stricter propagation, pair this with Experimental only evidence + Single-copy orthogroups.

How is OrthoGather different from PAINT?

PAINT is a desktop Java tool used by the GO Consortium's biocurators (humans, with biology backgrounds) to manually curate phylogenetic annotations across protein families. The result of their work is the IBA evidence code that you see in GOA files.

OrthoGather doesn't replace PAINT; it uses PAINT-curated annotations when you select the "Curated" evidence preset. PAINT is upstream curation; OrthoGather is downstream analysis.

What do the GO evidence codes mean?

Experimental: EXP (Inferred from Experiment), IDA (Direct Assay), IPI (Physical Interaction), IMP (Mutant Phenotype), IGI (Genetic Interaction), IEP (Expression Pattern). High-throughput experimental: HEP, HDA, HMP, HGI. Author statement: TAS (Traceable), NAS (Non-traceable). Curator: IC (Inferred by Curator). Phylogenetic: IBA (Inferred from Biological aspect of Ancestor, i.e. PAINT). Computational: IEA (Inferred from Electronic Annotation).

The full reference is at geneontology.org.

Per protein vs per orthogroup: which should I use?

If your foreground has many large orthogroups, per-protein counting can inflate significance (the same annotation gets counted N times, once per orthologous copy). Per-orthogroup counting is more conservative; each OG contributes one observation. We default to per-protein for compatibility with published workflows; switch to per-orthogroup when you want to be statistically careful.
